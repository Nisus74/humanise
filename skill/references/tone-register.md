# Tone and register

Channel tells you the format. Tone tells you the temperature. The same news ("we slipped the launch a week") reads differently in a board paper, a Slack message, and a customer email, and the difference isn't just length. This file is the dial: how warm, how blunt, how light, how formal, and how to move along each axis without losing the user's voice.

Two rules sit above everything here:

1. **Tone never licenses slop.** A warmer or funnier register still gets the full sweep. Humour is not an excuse for a throwaway slop opener; warmth is not an excuse for hedging.
2. **Match the reader's state, not a default.** The right tone is a function of what the reader is feeling when they open the message: anxious about a slip, excited about a win, sceptical of a pitch. Write to that, not to a house style.

---

## The formality ladder

Five rungs. Most of the user's writing sits on rungs 2 to 4. Knowing the rung stops you defaulting to a flat professional middle that reads as nobody in particular.

**Rung 1, raw.** Internal Slack, a note to a co-founder. Dropped subjects, sentence fragments where the rhythm calls for it, no sign-off. "Pushed it to Wednesday. QA found a calibration regression, fixing first."

**Rung 2, direct.** Internal email, a message to a trusted advisor. Full sentences, still conversational, contractions throughout, the ask up front. "We're slipping the firmware update to Wednesday. QA caught a calibration regression and I'd rather fix it than ship on schedule."

**Rung 3, considered.** External partner email, investor update, a customer the relationship is warm with. Cleaner sentences, fewer fragments, the same directness with a touch more care. Contractions still present. "The firmware update moves to Wednesday. QA found a calibration regression in testing, and we've held the release until it's resolved. No impact to the pilot timeline."

**Rung 4, formal.** Board paper, regulator-facing document, a first-contact procurement email. Full formality, minimal contractions, precise nouns, no slang. Still not stiff: the goal is the register of a senior person being precise, not a template. "The v2 firmware release has moved to 11 June following a calibration regression identified in QA. The pilot timeline is unaffected; the revalidation is scoped and underway."

**Rung 5, ceremonial.** Rare. A formal letter, a published statement, legal-adjacent prose. Used only when the occasion genuinely demands it. Over-reaching to rung 5 when rung 4 would do is its own tell; it reads as trying too hard.

**Moving between rungs.** To go up a rung: lengthen sentences slightly, swap slang for plain precision, reduce (don't eliminate) contractions, name things more formally. To go down: shorten, add a contraction, drop the subject on a sentence or two, let one fragment land. Don't change the _content_ or the _opinion_ as you move; only the surface temperature.

## Warmth

Warmth is how much the writing acknowledges the person on the other end. It's mostly carried by small moves, not by adjectives.

**Cold (avoid as a default):** "Please find the revised proposal attached. Feedback by Friday." Reads as transactional, even slightly cold, unless the relationship is purely transactional.

**Warm, still direct (usual target):** "Revised proposal attached. I took your point about the pilot scope and tightened it. Could you get me feedback by Friday?" The warmth is in naming what the reader contributed and asking rather than instructing.

**Over-warm (avoid):** "I hope you're having a wonderful week! I'm so thrilled to share the revised proposal, which I think you'll absolutely love." Effusive warmth reads as either AI or sales. the user's warmth is dry and specific, not gushing.

The reliable warm moves: acknowledge what the reader said or did, name them or their context specifically, ask instead of instruct where the ask is genuinely optional, and admit a constraint honestly rather than papering over it. Warmth comes from being _seen by_ the writer, not from nice words.

## Dry humour

the user's humour is dry, sparing, and load-bearing when it appears. It works by understatement and by naming the thing everyone's thinking, not by jokes.

**The understatement.** "That governance layer took longer to get right than the models did." The humour is in how much work "longer" is doing.

**The honest aside.** "We'd built it in three weeks, which is roughly three weeks longer than we'd planned." The parenthetical admits the slip without drama.

**The deadpan reframe.** "The AI was the exciting part. The hard part was commercial." Naming the less glamorous thing as the real one is the move.

What to avoid: puns, exclamation marks doing comedic work, "well, that happened" internet cadence, and humour that performs its own cleverness. If a line needs a reader to notice how clever it is, cut it. Dry humour lands because it almost isn't there.

Humour is optional and channel-dependent. Fine in a LinkedIn post, a blog, a warm email, internal Slack. Out of place in a board paper, a regulator submission, a first-contact procurement email. When in doubt, leave it out; a missed joke costs nothing, a misjudged one costs credibility.

---

## Bluntness

Bluntness is how directly the writing states a hard thing. the user defaults blunt-but-fair: name the problem plainly, don't soften it into mush, don't perform brutality either.

**Too soft (avoid):** "There may be some opportunities to revisit aspects of the current approach, which could potentially yield improvements." Says nothing, commits to nothing.

**Blunt and fair (target):** "The current approach won't scale past 10 labs. We'd be rebuilding it inside a year, so I'd rather change it now." States the problem, the consequence, and the call.

**Needlessly harsh (avoid):** "This approach is obviously wrong and whoever designed it didn't think it through." Bluntness about ideas, not people. The target is the decision, never the person who made it.

The blunt move that signals a real human: state the disagreement directly, then leave room. "The standard advice is X. I think that's wrong because Y. If I'm missing something, tell me." Conviction plus an open door reads as a confident person, not a hedger and not a bully.

## Putting it together: reading the reader's state

Before drafting, ask what the reader feels on opening this. Then set the dials.

- **Reader is anxious** (a slip, a risk, bad news): rung 3 to 4, warm, blunt about the facts, no humour. Lead with the situation and the plan. Don't bury the bad news and don't over-apologise.
- **Reader is excited** (a win, a launch): match some energy but undercut the hype. Rung 2 to 3, warm, a dry note keeps it from reading as a press release. State the number, not the adjective.
- **Reader is sceptical** (a pitch, a hard ask): rung 3 to 4, blunt, specific, humour off. Lead with the strongest concrete fact. Acknowledge the obvious objection before they raise it.
- **Reader is a peer in the trenches** (Slack, internal): rung 1 to 2, warm, dry humour welcome, maximum directness. Drop the subject, skip the sign-off.

When the channel playbook and this file seem to disagree, the reader's state wins. A board paper is rung 4 by default, but a board paper delivering genuinely bad news leans warmer and blunter than the default, because that's what the reader needs.
