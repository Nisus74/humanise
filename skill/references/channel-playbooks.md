# Channel playbooks

Each channel has its own shape. A LinkedIn post and a board paper are both professional writing but have nothing else in common. This file covers the channels the user writes in most, with specific guidance on what works for each, plus a mapping table at the end for everything unlisted.

Apply the general rules in `SKILL.md` always. Apply the channel-specific guidance here as an overlay.

## LinkedIn post

**Audience:** Product and tech network, investors, hiring pools, industry peers.
**Length:** 60–200 words. Longer reads as a blog post; shorter feels empty.
**Opening:** A specific observation from real work. Not a hook. Not a question. Not "Here's what I learned..." Start with the concrete.
**Body:** Tell one story or make one argument. Two or three short paragraphs. Prose, not bullets.
**Closing:** Specific takeaway tied to the story. No generic wisdom. No "What do you think?" bait unless the question is genuine.
**Rhythm:** Mix short and long sentences. A one-line sentence for punch works on LinkedIn if earned; stacking three one-liners doesn't.
**Traps to avoid:** Dramatic fragmentation ("Ship. Learn. Repeat."), binary contrasts ("It's not about X, it's about Y"), sentence templates ("The best PMs don't just... they..."), emoji-heavy formatting, bullet lists for everything.
**Calibration:** The reader should feel they learned something specific to your work, not something you could have read in a management book.

## Email: external

**Audience:** Investors, customers, partners, recruiters, regulators. People outside your company who decide based on the email.
**Length:** 50–150 words for routine; 200–400 for substantive. Over 400 should usually be a doc with a cover email.
**Opening:** The purpose of the email in the first sentence. No "I hope this finds you well." The person knows they're receiving an email; they want to know why.
**Body:** One to three short paragraphs. Each with a single point. If you're making three points, use a short numbered list rather than cramming them into prose.
**Closing:** Specific ask or specific next step. "Can you review by Friday?" beats "Let me know your thoughts." Offer a concrete alternative if the ask is optional.
**Subject line:** Specific enough to be useful when searched six months later. "[your company] Q2 pilot proposal, draft for your review" beats "Proposal".
**Traps to avoid:** "Circling back", "touching base", "just wanted to", "per my last email", "kindly", "hope this finds you well". All slop.
**Register tip:** Match the recipient's formality. Australian recipient: slightly informal is fine. US healthcare procurement: a touch more formal.

## Email: internal

**Audience:** your company team, advisors, directly involved parties.
**Length:** As short as possible. 20–80 words for most; Slack would usually be better if it's under 20.
**Opening:** Straight to the point. "Quick question about..." "Heads up on..." "Flagging..." are fine internal openers.
**Body:** One point per email. If there are two unrelated points, send two emails.
**Closing:** Direct ask or flag. "Can you handle?" "No action needed." "Will chase if I don't hear back."
**Traps to avoid:** Over-formality. Hedging. Apologising for writing ("Sorry to bother you, just wanted to..."). the user doesn't do this; don't insert it.

## Slack message

**Audience:** Team, advisors, close collaborators.
**Length:** 1–3 sentences most of the time. 4-plus sentences means you should be using threads.
**Opening:** Zero warm-up. First sentence is the content.
**Body:** Use threads for follow-ups. Use code blocks for commands and file paths. Mention people directly (@name) rather than "whoever's watching".
**Closing:** Usually none. If you're asking for a response, end with the question.
**Emoji:** the user uses them sparingly but does use them. OK in Slack; not elsewhere.
**Traps to avoid:** Formality. Bullet lists for two items. Long preambles. "So basically what I was thinking..."
**Tone shift:** This is the most casual of the user's channels. Contractions always. Dropped subjects occasionally fine ("Think we should push to Monday").

## Investor update

**Audience:** Cap-table investors, close advisors, sometimes prospective investors.
**Length:** 400–900 words. Long enough to show substance; short enough to be read.
**Opening:** Headline statement of what happened since last update. "We signed the a hospital network pilot" beats "Hope you had a great month!"
**Structure:** Five sections work well. Headlines (3–5 bullets of what happened), Metrics (specific numbers), Wins, Losses/Challenges (the honest ones), Asks. Adjust to what's relevant.
**Losses section:** Most important. An update with no losses section reads as marketing. the user includes at least one real challenge per update.
**Metrics:** Actual numbers, not adjectives. Period-over-period comparisons help.
**Asks:** Specific. "Intros to pathology directors in California, particularly contract labs doing over 500 tests/day" beats "Any intros appreciated".
**Closing:** Date of next update. Explicit ask if one exists.
**Traps to avoid:** Hype language, round-trip claims without data, vague mentions of "conversations", excessive exclamation, forward-looking statements that are actually hopes.

## PRD or product spec

**Audience:** Engineering, design, QA, sometimes stakeholders.
**Length:** Fits the problem. A small feature PRD might be 300 words; a platform change 3000.
**Opening:** Problem statement in the first paragraph. Who the user is, what they're trying to do, why the current state fails them. No "Background" section that introduces nothing.
**Structure:** Problem → proposed solution → scope (in/out) → success criteria → open questions. Vary to fit. Keep the open questions visible.
**Voice:** Direct and opinionated. PRDs that read as neutral "one could consider" documents don't drive decisions. the user takes a position.
**Assumptions:** Stated explicitly with "Assumes: ..." markers. Cheaper than finding out in review that two people had different mental models.
**Traps to avoid:** Front-loading context the reader doesn't need, burying the proposal under ten paragraphs of framing, hedging every claim, using "we" when you mean "I".
**Technical detail:** Include enough to make the decision; link to deeper docs for implementation. PRDs aren't design docs.

## Board paper

**Audience:** your company board, observers.
**Length:** 2–6 pages. Board time is expensive. Write to be read in 10 minutes.
**Opening:** One-paragraph executive summary. What's the state, what decision is sought.
**Structure:** Exec summary → state of the business → decisions sought → appendices. Metrics in the exec summary; supporting detail in the appendices.
**Decisions sought:** Stated explicitly. "Board to approve allocating $X to Y" or "Board to note Z". If you're not asking for a decision, say why you're bringing it.
**Risks:** Top three named, with mitigation and residual risk. Don't bury the risks in body text.
**Tone:** Professional but direct. The board has seen hype before; they discount it. Numbers carry more weight than adjectives.
**Traps to avoid:** Long preamble before the ask, over-formality, passive voice throughout, hiding bad news in the middle.

## Blog post or article

**Audience:** Public readers who found the post via search, share, or newsletter.
**Length:** 600–1500 words typical. Shorter for observations; longer for argued positions.
**Opening:** Specific moment, question, or claim. Not "In today's evolving landscape..." Not "We've all heard that..."
**Structure:** Set up the problem or observation; develop it; land a specific conclusion. A good blog post has one main argument, supported by two or three examples.
**Voice:** More editorial than other channels. A blog post without a position is a Wikipedia article without citations: valueless.
**Specifics:** Every claim tied to something concrete: a number, a person's name (with permission), a project, a date.
**Closing:** Specific takeaway, a question, or a forward-looking statement tied to what you just argued. No generic sign-offs.
**Traps to avoid:** Dramatic fragmentation, sentence templates, headline-style H2s ("The Power of X"), the five-paragraph-essay structure everyone learned in school.

## Status update

**Audience:** the user's direct line (board, CEO, co-founders, team leads).
**Length:** 150–400 words typical.
**Opening:** Biggest thing first. "Shipped sensor calibration v2 to the RPA pilot; first data coming in Monday" beats "This week the team worked on a variety of things."
**Structure:** Shipped → In flight → Blocked → Next. The "Blocked" section is where honesty shows; it's what distinguishes a useful update from a PR update.
**Numbers:** Always. Dates always. Names always.
**Traps to avoid:** Activity theatre (listing every meeting attended as if that's progress), hedging blockers ("might be a small delay"), burying bad news at the bottom.

## Release notes or changelog

**Audience:** Users and internal teams.
**Length:** One line per change, plus a short intro if there's a theme.
**Format:** Past tense. Subject-verb-object. Bulleted list grouped by type (Added / Changed / Fixed / Removed) or by area.
**Opening:** One-line release summary. Optional.
**Voice:** Plain. No marketing. Version "2.3" doesn't need to "Revolutionise Your Workflow." It just needs to say what changed.
**Traps to avoid:** Marketing copy dressed as release notes ("Excited to announce..."), vague "improvements", hiding breaking changes in "Changed".

## Proposal or RFP response

**Audience:** Procurement, technical evaluators, decision-makers.
**Length:** Whatever the RFP specifies. Minimum to answer fully; no more.
**Opening:** Direct response to the ask. No "Thank you for the opportunity..." preamble unless explicitly required.
**Structure:** Follow the RFP's structure exactly. If the RFP has sections A-G, your response has sections A-G in the same order with the same names.
**Voice:** Confident, specific. Every capability claim tied to evidence (case study, reference customer, spec sheet).
**Boilerplate:** Keep to a minimum. One paragraph of company context, not three.
**Closing:** Specific next step (proposed kick-off date, clarification call offer).
**Traps to avoid:** Marketing copy where a direct answer is called for, restating the question before answering, hedging capabilities.

## Customer support or pilot update

**Audience:** Pilot site (lab manager, director, clinical lead), customer contact.
**Length:** 80–250 words typical.
**Opening:** What's new, what's fixed, what needs their input. In that order.
**Voice:** Warm but not saccharine. Professional but not corporate. The customer is a person with a job; respect their time.
**Specifics:** Dates, version numbers, tickets, issues referenced by ID. "Issue #142 resolved" beats "We've addressed your concern."
**Closing:** Clear next step and timeline.
**Traps to avoid:** Over-apologising, vague timelines ("shortly", "soon"), hiding escalations, corporate-support language ("Thank you for reaching out. We value your feedback!").

## Conference talk abstract or speaker bio

**Audience:** Event organisers, conference attendees.
**Length:** Abstract 100–250 words. Bio 60–120 words.
**Opening (abstract):** The question the talk answers, or the surprising claim the talk makes. Not a preamble about the topic area.
**Body (abstract):** Specific points the talk will cover, in the order covered. Three or four is typical. Avoid vague "will explore".
**Bio:** Third person, factual, current role first. One or two specific credentials. Skip the "passionate about" adjectives.
**Voice:** Slightly more formal than LinkedIn but less than a board paper.
**Traps to avoid:** Abstract-as-marketing ("Join us as we explore..."), bio as career list without a through-line, generic "thought leader" language.

## Academic or grant writing

**Audience:** Grant assessors, ethics committees, research collaborators, journal reviewers. Often a panel scoring against criteria.
**Length:** Bound by the form. Respect word limits exactly; assessors read dozens and notice padding.
**Opening:** The specific problem and why it matters now, stated plainly. Not "research has long shown that". Lead with the gap your work fills.
**Body:** Claim, then evidence, then citation. Precision over flourish. The register is formal (rung 4) but the enemy is still slop: "novel", "paradigm-shifting", "cutting-edge", and "robust" are as hollow here as in marketing. Replace with the specific method, the specific n, the specific result.
**Structure:** Follow the funder's or journal's required headings exactly. Assessors score section by section; a missing required section loses marks regardless of quality.
**Citations:** Match the required style precisely and don't editorialise inside a citation.
**Closing:** What the funding or publication enables, concretely. Avoid "this work will pave the way for".
**Traps to avoid:** Significance inflation ("marking a pivotal advance"), hedging sandwiches ("it could be argued that this may potentially"), false ranges ("from molecules to populations"), and the passive-voice fog that academic writing invites. State who did what. Active voice is allowed in modern academic writing and reads as more confident.
**Calibration:** An assessor should finish each section knowing exactly what you did and why it matters, with no sentence they'd have to read twice to parse.

## Sales and marketing copy

**Audience:** Prospects, often skimming, often sceptical, sometimes running the copy through an AI detector before they reply.
**Length:** As short as the job allows. Landing-page sections in tens of words; a cold email under 120.
**Opening:** A specific, concrete benefit or observation, not a hook. "Your lab spends a shift a week reconciling calibration logs by hand" beats "Imagine a world where compliance is effortless."
**Body:** One claim, backed by a specific (a number, a named customer, a concrete outcome). Marketing copy is where slop concentrates most, so the sweep matters most here: "seamless", "robust", "transformative", "cutting-edge", "unlock", "empower", "best-in-class" are the native tongue of bad copy. Strip every one and say the specific thing instead.
**Closing:** One clear action. "Book a 20-minute technical call" beats "Reach out to learn more".
**Register:** Confident, specific, never hyped. The most persuasive copy reads like a knowledgeable person telling you something true, not a brochure.
**Detection note:** This is the channel most likely to be run through an AI detector by a buyer. Drop even load-bearing binary contrasts here; keep it clean of every syntactic tell.
**Traps to avoid:** Manufactured urgency ("Don't miss out"), false exclusivity ("the secret top labs use"), the rule of three in every headline, and benefit-free adjective stacks. If a sentence would describe any competitor's product, it isn't selling yours.

## Executive memo

**Audience:** A CEO, a peer executive, or an exec team. Time-poor, decision-oriented, allergic to padding.
**Length:** One page, ideally less. If it runs past a page it should have a one-paragraph summary at the top.
**Opening:** The recommendation or the decision needed, in the first two sentences. The exec reads the top and decides whether to read on; earn it.
**Structure:** Recommendation first, then the two or three reasons, then the relevant detail, then the ask. The inverse of academic structure: conclusion up front, support below.
**Body:** Each paragraph one idea. Numbers where they carry the argument. Cut the narrative throat-clearing; an exec doesn't need the journey.
**Closing:** The specific decision or action sought, with a date. "I need a yes/no on the $200k by Friday to hold the California timeline" beats "Let me know your thoughts."
**Register:** Rung 3 to 4. Direct, confident, no hedging. An exec memo that hedges reads as someone who hasn't decided what they think.
**Traps to avoid:** Burying the recommendation, listing options without a recommendation, over-context (the exec has context), and false balance that avoids making a call.

## Press release or PR statement

**Audience:** Journalists, the public, sometimes regulators and partners reading for tone. Quoted verbatim, so every word is on the record.
**Length:** A release runs 300–500 words; a statement can be two or three sentences.
**Opening:** The news, factually, in the first sentence. Who, what, when. Not "We are thrilled to announce".
**Body:** Facts first, then a quote that says something a person would actually say. The classic PR quote ("We are excited to leverage this transformative partnership to deliver best-in-class value") is pure slop and reads as such; a real quote makes a specific point or claim. Attribute it properly.
**Register:** Formal (rung 4) but human. The trap is corporate-release boilerplate, which is slop with a letterhead.
**On the record:** Assume every sentence could be quoted out of context. Avoid hostages to fortune ("this will revolutionise the industry") and anything you couldn't defend to a regulator.
**Traps to avoid:** "Thrilled/excited/delighted to announce" openers, hype adjectives in quotes, vague future promises, and exclamation marks. For a regulated company, also avoid claims that could read as a clinical or efficacy promise unless they're substantiated and cleared.

## LinkedIn DM or cold outreach

**Audience:** Candidates, potential partners, intro targets, conference contacts. People who get twenty of these a week and delete nineteen.
**Length:** Under 90 words; a first message ideally under 60. If it scrolls, it's dead.
**Opening:** The specific reason it's this person. "You've run NATA assessments for contract labs" or "Your talk on LIMS migrations at APP last year". A reason that would survive being read aloud to them. Generic personalisation ("I came across your profile") is worse than none.
**Body:** One ask, sized small. A 15-minute call, one question, one intro. Don't pitch the product in message one; the ask is the conversation, the conversation carries the pitch.
**Closing:** Low-pressure and specific. "Keen to chat if you're open to it" with a concrete option beats "Let me know your availability".
**Traps to avoid:** Flattery openers ("I love what you're building!"), the connect-then-pitch-slap sequence, fake familiarity, "quick question" as a hook with a long message underneath, and AI-template personalisation ("I was impressed by your recent post about X"). This channel is drowning in machine-written outreach; the only differentiator is sounding like a person who did five minutes of real homework.
**Register:** Rung 2–3. AU register for AU recipients; lift one notch for US.

## LinkedIn comment or reply

**Audience:** The post author plus everyone who reads the thread. Public, attributed, permanent.
**Length:** One to three sentences. Longer belongs in your own post.
**The bar:** Add something the post didn't have: a number from your own work, a specific counter-example, a sharper version of the point. If the comment would work under any post on the topic, don't post it.
**Disagreeing:** Name it plainly and leave room, the standard move: "We saw the opposite at our pilot sites; buy-in beat integration every time. Curious if lab size explains the difference."
**Traps to avoid:** Restating the post back ("So true that..."), congratulation-stacking ("Huge! Well deserved!"), "This. 100%", emoji strings, engagement-bait questions, and turning a comment into a pitch.
**Register:** Rung 2. Contractions on, humour allowed if dry.

## Slide copy

**Audience:** A room (or a screen-share) reading while someone talks. Words compete with a voice.
**Headlines:** Assertion headlines that carry the argument, not topic labels. "Calibration drift caught within 4 hours" beats "Calibration: overview". A reader skimming only headlines should get the whole argument.
**Bullets:** Fragments, around six words, no full stops. Parallel structure is acceptable here; bullets are a scanning format and the triple rule relaxes. The slop vocabulary rules don't relax: "robust", "seamless" and friends are as dead on a slide as in prose.
**Numbers:** Numbers over adjectives, always. One number per slide gets remembered; six get ignored.
**Density:** One idea per slide. If a slide needs a paragraph, the paragraph belongs in the appendix or the doc, and the slide gets the conclusion.
**Speaker notes:** Full prose, full sweep, written at the rung of the room.
**Tooling note:** For your company decks, the airlabone-deck skill owns layout and brand; this playbook owns the words.

## Job ad and candidate outreach

**Audience:** Engineers and product people who read job ads with a slop detector built in. The good ones have options and skim.
**Opening:** The actual work, first two sentences. "You'll own the sensor-ingest pipeline that twelve pathology labs depend on" beats any amount of "About us". Company context comes after the work, one short paragraph.
**Body:** What they'll build (named problems, named stack), what's hard about it stated honestly (one real difficulty: regulated environment, on-prem constraints, solo-to-team transition), and who they'll work with. The honest-difficulty line does more recruiting work than every perk combined, because nobody else writes it.
**Salary:** Range in the ad wherever possible. Omitting it costs candidates and signals games.
**Closing:** How to apply, what the process is, and a response-time commitment you'll keep.
**Traps to avoid:** "Rockstar/ninja/guru", "fast-paced environment", "wear many hats", "passionate about", values wallpaper, and requirement lists that describe three different people. If a sentence would fit any startup's ad, cut it.
**Candidate outreach (DM/email):** Use the LinkedIn DM playbook plus one specific thing from their background that's actually relevant. Name the role, the comp range if asked, and the one reason this role beats their current one. Never mass-personalise.

## Customer incident comms

**Audience:** Lab manager, director, clinical lead, mid-incident. Anxious, time-poor, regulated, and keeping your message for the audit file.
**Structure, in order:** What happened. Impact scope (which results, samples, or instruments are affected, and explicitly which are not). What we're doing. What you need to do. When the next update comes.
**Opening:** Impact scope, plainly, first sentence. "Calibration records for sensors at your site between 02:00 and 06:00 today may be affected; results released before 02:00 are not" beats any preamble.
**Cadence:** Time-stamped updates at the promised interval even when nothing changed: "No change since 14:00; next update 16:00." Silence reads as chaos.
**Contact:** A named human with a direct channel, not a ticket queue.
**Regulatory:** If results integrity is touched, state what's quarantined and the revalidation plan. Assume every sentence lands in front of a NATA assessor later; write it so that's fine.
**Traps to avoid:** Minimising ("a small glitch"), speculating on cause before it's confirmed, blame-shifting ("a third-party vendor issue"), over-apologising, and vague timelines ("shortly"). One sincere acknowledgement, then facts and plan.
**Register:** Rung 4, warm, blunt about facts, zero humour. The reader-state rules in `tone-register.md` (anxious reader) apply in full.

## Using this file

When starting a draft, identify the channel and read the relevant playbook. Apply the channel-specific guidance on top of the general rules in `SKILL.md`. If the channel isn't listed here and isn't in the mapping table, pick the closest one and flag the gap.

The playbooks are heuristics, not hard rules. the user's specific voice (in `profile/voice-fingerprint.md`) overrides general channel advice where the two conflict. If the fingerprint shows they end board papers differently to this playbook, follow the fingerprint.
